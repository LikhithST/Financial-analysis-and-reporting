# Lecture 03: Accounting Concepts

## Accounting Conventions

Accounting conventions are commonly accepted practices that guide the preparation of financial statements.

1. Consistency
2. Disclosure
3. Conservatism (Prudence)
4. Materiality

### 1. Convention of Consistency

Once a business adopts an accounting method, it should continue to use that method consistently. A change should be made only when there is a sound reason to do so.

#### Example: Depreciation

Depreciation is the reduction in the value of an asset because of wear and tear, usage, or the passage of time. Businesses record depreciation in their books of accounts using a selected method.

Common methods include:

1. Straight-Line Method (SLM), also called the Fixed Instalment Method
2. Diminishing Balance Method (DBM), also called the Written-Down Value (WDV) Method

If a business uses one depreciation method, it should continue using it unless there is a valid reason to change.

### 2. Convention of Disclosure

All material information necessary for users to understand financial statements should be disclosed.

For example, if a business changes its depreciation method, it must disclose the change and its effect on the accounts.

### 3. Convention of Conservatism (Prudence)

Conservatism means that anticipated losses should be recognised, while anticipated profits should not be recognised until they are actually earned.

- Even if a business is highly confident that it will earn a profit from a transaction, that profit is not recorded until it is earned.
- If there is a potential loss, the business considers its effect promptly and takes necessary measures.

In short: recognise expected losses, but do not recognise expected profits.

### 4. Convention of Materiality

Only information significant enough to influence decisions needs separate recognition in the books of accounts. Small, similar expenses may be grouped under one appropriate heading to simplify record-keeping.

#### Example: Stationery

Pens, pencils, paper, and similar minor items can be recorded together under the single head **Stationery**, rather than as many separate transactions. This reduces unnecessary detail while retaining material information.

## Accounting Process

The accounting process generally follows these stages:

1. Transaction
2. Journal
3. Ledger
4. Trial Balance
5. Income Statement
6. Balance Sheet
7. Fund Flow Statement
8. Cash Flow Statement

An additional statement that may be prepared is the **Profit and Loss Appropriation Account**.

### 1. Transaction

A transaction is an event that has a financial effect on the business, such as money or value moving into or out of the business.

### 2. Journal

A journal is the first, or original, book of accounting. Transactions are first recorded here in chronological order.

**Basic journal format:**

| Particulars | LF (Ledger Folio) | Debit Amount | Credit Amount |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
|  |  |  |  |

### 3. Ledger

Transactions recorded in the journal are posted to the ledger. The ledger contains separate accounts for similar transactions, and these accounts are balanced periodically.

Accounts are broadly classified as:

- Personal accounts
- Real accounts
- Nominal accounts

Examples of real accounts include cash, buildings, and materials—assets that can be seen, observed, or touched. To determine the cash balance, a cash account is prepared and balanced in the ledger.

**Basic ledger format:**

| Debit Particulars | Journal Folio | Debit Amount | Credit Particulars | Journal Folio | Credit Amount |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- | --- | ---: |
|  |  |  |  |  |  |

### 4. Trial Balance

A trial balance is an interim statement prepared after ledger accounts have been balanced and before preparing the income statement and balance sheet.

**Basic trial balance format:**

| Head of Account | Debit Balance | Credit Balance |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
|  |  |  |

It is used to check that transactions have been accurately recorded in the journal, correctly posted to the ledger, and are free from arithmetic errors.

## Clarification: Income Statement and Profit and Loss Account

In most introductory accounting contexts, the **income statement** and **profit and loss account** refer to the same statement: a report of a business's revenues, expenses, and resulting profit or loss for a period.

The term *profit and loss account* is traditional, while *income statement* is the more modern and widely used term. Depending on the accounting framework, an income statement may also include other comprehensive income or be presented as part of a broader statement of profit or loss.
