# Lecture 4: Trial Balance and Its Relevance

A balanced trial balance is used to prepare the two primary financial statements:

- the Trading and Profit and Loss Account; and
- the Balance Sheet.

Together with the Cash Flow Statement, these are the statutory statements used for financial reporting.

## 1. Trading and Profit and Loss Account

The Trading and Profit and Loss Account summarises a business's expenses and income for an accounting period, usually 12 months. Its debit and credit totals are compared to determine profit or loss.

**Example heading:** *Trading and Profit and Loss Account of XYZ Ltd. for the year ending 31 December 2010.*

### 1.1 Trading Account

The Trading Account compares direct income with direct expenses to calculate gross profit.

| Debit (Dr.) | Amount | Credit (Cr.) | Amount |
| --- | ---: | --- | ---: |
| To Direct Expenses |  | By Sales |  |
| To Gross Profit (G/P) |  | By Closing Stock |  |

Expenses are introduced with **To** on the debit side, while income is introduced with **By** on the credit side.

#### Direct income

1. Sales income.
2. Value of closing stock, which will generate income when it is sold in a future period.

#### Direct expenses

Direct expenses are costs without which a finished product cannot be produced. Typical examples are:

1. Raw materials — approximately 60% of production cost.
2. Direct wages — wages for daily-wage production workers; salaries of permanent employees are generally indirect expenses — approximately 25%.
3. Other direct overheads, such as power, lubricants, and water — approximately 15%.

When credit-side direct income exceeds debit-side direct expenses, the difference is **gross profit**. It is recorded on the debit side as *To Gross Profit* so that the account balances.

### 1.2 Profit and Loss Account

The Profit and Loss Account compares indirect expenses with gross profit and other income to calculate net profit.

| Debit (Dr.) | Amount | Credit (Cr.) | Amount |
| --- | ---: | --- | ---: |
| To Indirect Expenses |  | By Gross Profit (G/P) |  |
| To Net Profit (N/P) |  | By Other Income |  |

Gross profit from the Trading Account is transferred to the credit side of the Profit and Loss Account as *By Gross Profit*. It is income available to cover indirect expenses and earn net profit.

Examples of indirect expenses include salaries of white-collar employees, office expenses, telephone, insurance, and depreciation.

When the credit total exceeds the debit total, the difference is **net profit before tax**. After corporate tax is paid, the result is **net operating profit after tax (NOPAT)**. The balancing net-profit figure is shown on the debit side as *To Net Profit*.

At the end of the 12-month accounting period, the business begins the next accounting period.

### 1.3 Characteristics of the Profit and Loss Account

- It is a nominal account.
- Expenses decrease capital; income increases capital.
- Income is shown on the credit side and expenses on the debit side.
- It has no opening balance, but it closes with a profit or loss balance.

## 2. Balance Sheet

The Balance Sheet reports the financial position of a business on a specific date, rather than for a period.

**Example heading:** *Balance Sheet of XYZ Ltd. as at 31 December 2010.*

| Liabilities and Capital | Amount | Assets | Amount |
| --- | ---: | --- | ---: |
| Share Capital |  | Fixed Assets |  |
| Add: Net Profit |  | |  |
| Reserves and Surplus |  |  |  |
| Long-term Liabilities |  |  |  |
| Current Liabilities |  | Current Assets  |  |

The total assets must equal the total liabilities and capital:

> **Liabilities + Capital = Assets**

### 2.1 Liabilities and Capital

Liabilities represent the financial resources generated by a firm from different sources.

1. **Share capital / capital:** Funds invested by the owners. For example, if a business needs ₹100,000 and the owner contributes ₹30,000 while borrowing ₹70,000, both amounts are sources of funds for the business.
   - In a sole proprietorship, the owner and business have the same liability.
   - In a partnership, the owners and business have the same liability.
   - In a private or public limited company, liabilities belong to the company. Owners' liability is limited to the capital they have agreed to contribute.
2. **Net profit:** Net profit is added back to capital.
3. **Reserves and surplus:** A part of net profit retained for general purposes.
4. **Long-term liabilities:** Loans repayable after more than one year.
5. **Current liabilities:** Amounts borrowed or payable within one year.

### 2.2 Assets

1. **Fixed assets:** Assets used in the business for more than one year and on which depreciation is charged.
2. **Current assets:** Assets expected to be held or realised within 12 months, such as raw materials and receivables from credit sales (often collected within three months).

### 2.3 Other Balance Sheet Points

- A Balance Sheet may be prepared in T-format or vertical format.
- It shows the business's capital or general fund.
- Specific reserves may also be created from profits.
- If beginning capital is not given, it can be determined by preparing an opening Balance Sheet using the accounting equation.

## 3. Other Financial Statements

The three statutory statements used for reporting are:

1. Profit and Loss Statement
2. Balance Sheet
3. Cash Flow Statement

The Cash Flow Statement is prepared using information from the Profit and Loss Account and Balance Sheet.

### Profit and Loss Appropriation Account

A Profit and Loss Appropriation Account is prepared alongside the Profit and Loss Account. It helps determine how net profit will be allocated, including amounts for dividends, additional capital, general reserves, and specific reserves.
