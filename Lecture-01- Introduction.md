# Lecture 01: Introduction to Financial Statements and Accounting Systems

## Financial Statements

The three principal financial statements are:

1. **Trading and Profit & Loss Account**
2. **Balance Sheet**
3. **Cash Flow Statement**

## Accounting Systems

Two main systems are used to prepare financial statements:

1. **Cash system of accounting**
2. **Mercantile (accrual) system of accounting**

### Cash System of Accounting

Under the cash system, a business records transactions only when cash moves.

#### Examples

- When a firm purchases raw materials and pays cash, it records the purchase upon payment.
- When a firm sells finished goods entirely for cash, the goods move to customers and cash flows into the firm. There are no credit transactions or outstanding dues.

Only transactions that affect cash are recorded in the financial statements under this system.

### Mercantile (Accrual) System of Accounting

Under the mercantile, or accrual, system, recording a transaction does not depend on the movement of cash. Transactions are recognised when they occur.

#### Examples

- A firm may purchase raw materials now and pay its suppliers two months later. The purchase is still recorded when it occurs.
- A firm may sell finished products on credit. Once the sale has taken place and the product has been delivered to the buyer, the sale is recognised even though cash has not yet been received.

## Use in Financial Reporting

In India, the cash system is used only in the banking sector. For financial reporting in other sectors, businesses use the mercantile (accrual) system of accounting.

## Banking and Non-Performing Assets (NPAs)

In banking, the accrual system did not always present the true performance of a bank, particularly when borrowers stopped repaying loans.

> **Non-Performing Asset (NPA):** A loan on which the borrower is no longer making the required principal or interest payments.

For a bank:

- A **loan** is an asset.
- Customer **deposits** are liabilities.

### Illustration

Suppose a person borrows ₹1 million from Punjab National Bank (PNB). The borrower pays instalments regularly for one year and then stops repaying.

Under accrual accounting, the bank could continue to show the loan and its accrued interest as an asset, even though it is no longer receiving instalments. In reality, the loan has become a non-performing asset.

Banks therefore use the cash system for such recognition: loan interest and principal are treated as receipts only when they are actually paid to the bank. This ensures that only performing loans are reflected as good assets.
