# Lecture 02: The Accounting Process

## Key Term

**GAAP** stands for *Generally Accepted Accounting Principles*: a common set of accounting principles, standards, and procedures used to prepare financial statements.

## Double-Entry Accounting System (DEAS)

The **double-entry accounting system** records every business transaction with at least two effects. One or more accounts are debited and one or more accounts are credited, so that:

> **Total debits = Total credits**

For example, when a business buys raw materials for cash, materials enter the business and cash leaves it. The purchase/materials account is debited and the cash account is credited.

Transactions are first recorded in the **journal**, often called the first book of accounts. They are later classified in the ledger and ultimately reported in the financial statements, including the balance sheet.

### Historical Note

The double-entry system was documented by the Italian merchant and mathematician **Luca Pacioli** in *De Computis et Scripturis*.

### Single-Entry Accounting

**Single-entry accounting** records only one side of a transaction, commonly as a cash book or simple income-and-expense record. It may be used by very small organisations, but it does not provide the complete checks and financial reporting available under double-entry accounting.

## Types of Accounts

Traditional accounting classifies accounts into three groups:

| Account type | Includes | Traditional rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Personal account | Individuals and organisations, such as customers, suppliers, companies, and banks | Debit the receiver; credit the giver |
| Real account | Assets and property, such as cash, land, buildings, machinery, and furniture | Debit what comes in; credit what goes out |
| Nominal account | Expenses, losses, incomes, and gains | Debit expenses and losses; credit incomes and gains |

### Example: Purchase of Raw Materials for Cash

If XYZ Ltd. purchases raw materials worth ₹1,000 for cash:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Purchases/Raw Materials | ₹1,000 | — |
| Cash | — | ₹1,000 |

The debit and credit totals remain equal.

## Debit and Credit

Debit and credit are the two sides of an accounting entry. They do not automatically mean “good” or “bad,” nor do they simply mean money received or paid.

| Side | Usual effect |
| --- | --- |
| Debit | Increases assets and expenses; decreases liabilities, capital, and income |
| Credit | Increases liabilities, capital, and income; decreases assets and expenses |

The left side of an account is the **debit side**; the right side is the **credit side**.

## Accounting Cycle and Financial Statements

The accounting process begins with business transactions and moves from the journal to the ledger, trial balance, and financial statements.

The three principal financial statements are:

1. **Profit and Loss Statement** — reports income, expenses, and profit or loss for a period.
2. **Balance Sheet** — reports assets, liabilities, and capital/equity at a particular date.
3. **Cash Flow Statement** — explains changes in cash and cash equivalents using information from the profit and loss statement and balance sheet.

## Accounting Equation

The balance sheet is based on the accounting equation:

> **Assets = Liabilities + Capital (Equity)**

Every transaction is recorded so that this equation remains balanced. A difference between the two sides normally indicates an error or an incomplete accounting record; it is not a measure of whether the business is doing well or poorly.

## Accounting Concepts and Conventions

Accounting concepts are assumptions that guide the preparation of financial records and statements.

### 1. Business Entity Concept

The business is treated as separate from its owner(s). Business transactions must therefore be distinguished from personal transactions.

In a sole proprietorship, the owner and business may not be legally separate, but accounting still treats the business as a distinct entity. Companies have their own legal identity separate from shareholders.

### 2. Going Concern Concept

Financial statements assume that the business will continue operating for the foreseeable future, unless there is evidence to the contrary. Owners may change, but the business can continue.

### 3. Accrual Concept

Transactions are recorded when they occur, rather than only when cash is received or paid.

### 4. Money Measurement Concept

Only events that can be measured reliably in monetary terms are recorded in the accounts. For example, poor working relationships may affect a business but are not ordinarily recorded because they cannot be measured objectively in money.

### 5. Historical Cost Concept

Assets are generally recorded at their original purchase cost. For example, land bought for ₹100,000 is initially shown at ₹100,000, even if its market value later rises to ₹500,000, unless an applicable accounting standard permits or requires remeasurement.

### 6. Dual-Aspect Concept

Every transaction has at least two effects. This is the foundation of double-entry accounting: each debit has a corresponding credit.

### 7. Accounting Period Concept

Although a business may continue indefinitely, its performance is measured over defined periods—commonly 12 months—so that profit or loss can be reported regularly.

### 8. Matching Concept

Expenses should be recognised in the same accounting period as the revenue they helped generate. This helps determine the period’s profit or loss accurately.

### 9. Revenue Recognition (Realisation) Concept

Revenue is recognised when it is earned and the relevant performance obligation has been satisfied, rather than merely when cash is collected. For example, a credit sale is recorded when the goods are sold, even if payment is due later.

### 10. Objective Evidence Concept

Every accounting transaction should be supported by verifiable evidence, such as invoices, receipts, vouchers, bank statements, or purchase orders.

## Quick Reference

- **Sole proprietorship:** A business owned and controlled by one person. The owner is personally responsible for the business’s obligations, though accounting records still keep business and personal transactions separate.
- **Journal:** The first book of accounts, where transactions are initially recorded in chronological order.
- **Balance sheet:** A statement showing assets, liabilities, and capital/equity on a specific date.
