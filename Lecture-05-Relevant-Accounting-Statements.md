# Lecture 5: Relevant Accounting Statements and Books of Original Entry

## 1. Statutory Financial Statements

In India, the Cash Flow Statement has been a statutory financial statement since 1997, alongside the Profit and Loss Account and the Balance Sheet.

The key financial statements are:

1. Profit and Loss Account
2. Balance Sheet
3. Cash Flow Statement

## 2. Profit and Loss Account

The Profit and Loss (P&L) Account shows a business's profit or loss for an accounting period. It is used to determine net operating profit before tax and, after allowing for tax, net operating profit after tax (NOPAT).

## 3. Profit and Loss Appropriation Account

After tax, a company's profit may be allocated in several ways. For a public limited company, common appropriations include:

- **Dividends:** Amounts distributed to shareholders.
- **Specific reserves:** Amounts retained for a stated purpose.
- **General reserve:** Amounts retained for general business needs.
- **Retained earnings:** Any remaining profit transferred to the Balance Sheet and added to the owners' equity or capital.

This allocation is shown in the **Profit and Loss Appropriation Account**. It may be presented as an extension of the P&L Account or as a separate statement.

## 4. Cash Flow and Funds Flow Statements

### 4.1 Cash Flow Statement

A Cash Flow Statement records movements that affect cash and cash equivalents.

### 4.2 Funds Flow Statement

A Funds Flow Statement provides a broader view of changes in financial resources. Unlike a Cash Flow Statement, it considers changes in assets and liabilities beyond immediate cash movements. Over time, those items may be realised or settled in cash.

The Funds Flow Statement is not a statutory financial statement.

## 5. Books of Original Entry

Books of original entry (also called journals) record transactions before they are posted to the ledger.

### 5.1 Purchase Book

The Purchase Book records **credit purchases of goods for resale** (merchandise).

Merchandise is bought to be resold, usually after adding value. For example, stationery purchased for use in the office is not merchandise and is therefore not entered in the Purchase Book.

### 5.2 Sales Book

The Sales Book records credit sales of goods by the business.

### 5.3 Purchase Returns Book

The Purchase Returns Book records goods previously purchased that are returned to suppliers.

### 5.4 Sales Returns Book

The Sales Returns Book records goods returned by customers after a sale.

### 5.5 Bills Receivable Book

The Bills Receivable Book records bills received from customers. For example, when Firm A sells goods to Firm B on credit, Firm B may accept a legal document confirming that it will pay Firm A a specified amount after an agreed period, such as 30 or 40 days. For Firm A, this document is a bill receivable.

### 5.6 Bills Payable Book

The same document is a bill payable for Firm B, because Firm B is obliged to make the payment. The Bills Payable Book records such obligations.

### 5.7 Cash Book

The Cash Book records all cash transactions, whether or not they relate to merchandise.

### 5.8 Journal Proper

The Journal Proper records transactions that do not belong in the specialised journals above. For example:

- Cash purchase of stationery is recorded in the Cash Book.
- Credit purchase of stationery is recorded in the Journal Proper because the stationery is for office use, not resale.

## 6. Errors and the Trial Balance

Some accounting errors do not affect the agreement of debit and credit totals, so they cannot be detected by a Trial Balance. A Trial Balance verifies arithmetic accuracy only; it does not prove that every entry is correct.

### 6.1 Errors Not Detected by a Trial Balance

1. **Error of commission:** An amount is posted to the account of the wrong person, although the debit and credit totals remain equal.
2. **Error of omission:** A transaction is completely omitted from the books, so both its debit and credit entries are missing.
3. **Error of principle:** A transaction is recorded in the wrong type of account because an accounting principle is misunderstood, such as treating a capital expense as a revenue expense.

### 6.2 Capital and Revenue Expenditure

- **Capital expenditure** is spent to acquire, improve, or extend the useful life of a long-term asset. It provides benefits for more than one accounting period and is generally recorded as an asset. Examples include buying machinery or paying to install it.
- **Revenue expenditure** is incurred in the normal course of business to operate or maintain the business during the current period. It is generally recorded as an expense in the P&L Account. Examples include wages, rent, and routine repairs.

## 7. Suspense Account

If a Trial Balance does not agree, the difference may temporarily be placed in a **Suspense Account** so that the accounts can be finalised and the Balance Sheet prepared. Once the error is found and corrected, the Suspense Account is eliminated from the Trial Balance, Profit and Loss Account, and Balance Sheet.
